Does a tyre-degradation model transfer?

One protocol across four championships and seven car classes. 1,280 real pit-stop decisions replayed against it. Two of the three results are negative, and most of the third was the audit's own question.

Mohammed Reda Medjadj · Formula 1 · WEC · IMSA · ELMS

1Public timing rarely carries enough signal to test transfer

Fit a degradation slope on every season of a circuit but one, predict the held-out season, score the within-stint residual. Do it the same way everywhere.

+0.573best transfer anywhere — IMSA GTD at Lime Rock
5 of 51circuit-classes reaching R² = 0.2 at all

GT3 classes transfer measurably better than prototypes: difference +0.164, 95% CI [+0.059, +0.312], permutation p = 0.0011. Resampled at the circuit-class, never the fold — folds inside a class share a fitted slope.

The obvious explanation is performance-balanced machinery. It is ruled out: ELMS LMP2 is near-specification — one chassis, one engine, no Balance of Performance — and fails exactly as the balanced fields do. The instability is not the hardware.

Two repairs a team would try first, both tested on Formula 1. Coding each lap by the compound Pirelli actually supplied (C1–C6, 92 races) instead of Soft/Medium/Hard: at the 14 circuits whose allocation changed (degenerate fits excluded), the median paired change is −0.008 [−0.027, +0.001], worse at 10, and nothing is gained at the 7 unstable ones. Measuring the slope on Friday's long runs instead of other seasons: median R² −0.502 against +0.021, worse in 44 of 56 races. Neither helps. Past-only fitting costs a little. Where the laps carry signal, a circuit's own slope reaches a median 0.71 of the noise ceiling against 0.25 for a slope common to the category, a difference that points to the circuit but is not established.

2The stop cost and the tyre both set the regime; alone, the stop cost ranks it best

An exact dynamic program plans every endurance race under a hard fuel constraint and reports whether the tyre-optimal plan needs more stops than the tank forces.

−0.982pit loss vs tyre-limited share, 95% CI [−0.986, −0.745]
150 → 0race-seasons above a 22.5 s pit loss, and how many are tyre-limited

Monotonic across all six classes with no inversion. The interval is bootstrapped over races, recomputing each class summary inside every replicate — bootstrapping six fixed championships would produce an interval over a variation that does not exist.

The rule is robust; the constant is not. The 22.5 s edge is a maximum set by a single race — the next tyre-limited race sits at 13.2 s. No interval is reported for it, because a bootstrap of a maximum is degenerate on one side. Quote the rule; treat the number as an order of magnitude.

Both terms enter. In the closed-form index the slope carries more of the spread, and dropping either from it costs a similar share of the verdicts (difference +0.030 [−0.017, +0.084]). Alone, pit loss ranks the regimes better than the slope by +0.155 [+0.087, +0.231]. This describes the endurance planning model on these four championships, not a law.

3A planner stops later than teams do — and most of it was the question

Every real first stop is replayed. The model is asked five laps early, given the state as it was, and its recommendation compared to the lap the team chose.

Four explanations were tested, and none accounted for the magnitude.

No track position, so the engine cannot pay for an undercut. Re-running all 357 F1 decisions through a cover-aware Stackelberg engine moves the recommendation away from the real stop. Rejected.

Slopes biased toward durability. Against an independent source separating tyre wear from fuel burn by a different method, the paired difference is negligible — an error that size moves a stop by several race distances, not ten laps. Not detected.

Salminen's position-keeping defence. The right sign, and it fails on the quantity it depends on by a factor of 3.4. Too small.

A fresh tyre is worth more than the slope says. The most natural explanation of all. The residuals say the opposite: at the youngest measured tyre age laps are +0.187 s slower than the fit expects. That is warm-up, and pricing it in widens the gap. Wrong sign.

The fifth explanation was the audit itself. The planner plans one stop and runs the stint after it to the flag. For a car that really stopped again, that is not the question the pit wall answered. Replayed with each team's own next stop as the end of the stint, the Formula 1 gap falls from +10 laps to +2 [+1.0, +4.0], and no endurance class keeps an established gap.

That is agreement the window does not hand out for free: in Formula 1 the planner chooses from a median of 24 laps and misses the real stop by 3, where a lap picked at random from the same window would miss by 8.3. In WEC Hypercar and ELMS the fuel range leaves too few laps for the test to discriminate, and it is reported that way.

What survives is +4 laps [+2.0, +7.0] in one-stop races, where the planner and the team were answering the same question. Measured in the races, a lap of distance from the planner's lap costs 0.42 s [−0.24, +1.11], against 0.25 s by the planner's own pricing: the races cannot confirm or reject that price, with 5.6 s of spread between cars of the same race. The overall +2 holds asked 5, 10 or 15 laps ahead and for the whole field (+1 to +3); the one-stop residual is +1 to +4 depending on how early the planner is asked, and not established for the whole field asked 15 laps ahead (+1 [−1.0, +3.0]). Teams stop as if the slope ratio of new to worn tyre were 0.77 [0.70, 0.83] rather than the fitted 1.02 — possibly knowledge the public timing cannot resolve rather than an error. In endurance, with one net slope, the replay cannot test the tyre model. A prospective test was frozen before the 2026 São Paulo Grand Prix. It is the first of the three questions this project would most like answered.

And a rule of thumb beats it, in five of the seven classes

Three baselines, scored on 1,263 of the audit's 1,280 decisions, from the same artifacts, on the same metric. Written to win if they could — a straw man would prove nothing. The 17 that are not scored sit at four IMSA race-seasons with no fitted multi-stop plan, so there is no pit loss or net slope for a rule to read. Separately, a rule that cannot fire on a decision returns no lap and is not credited with one: coverage is reported beside every error, because answering a fifth of the decisions accurately is not the same as answering all of them.

Median absolute lap error against the real stop. Reported per class, because IMSA runs three at the same rounds with median pit losses of 57 s (GTP), 24 s (GTD) and 40 s (GTD PRO) — one IMSA row would average three strategy regimes into a number describing none of them. A rule sits closer than the optimiser in 5 of the 7 classes; in three of those the winning rule is B1, which uses no fitted quantity at all, and in the two others it ties the optimiser rather than losing to it. B3 is undefined in Formula 1, which has not refuelled since 2010, and is reported as undefined rather than substituted with the race end.

Closer to what teams did is not the same as better. Every number here scores agreement with practice, never which plan was faster. What it does settle is narrower and still worth having: the 12-lap gap cannot come from the simpler methods lacking information the optimiser has. They have less, and land nearer.

What underwrites it

No fabricated data, anywhere. Every quantity is measured from published timing or absent. Where a source lacks something, that is a stated limitation and the affected layer is not fitted.

Two estimators were withdrawn. A track-evolution correction was validated on synthetic races and failed twice on real fields. It is described rather than omitted, and the omitted variable is reported as diagnosed and unfixed.

The paper contains no numbers. Every quantity in the manuscript is a macro generated from the committed artifacts, so it cannot drift from the data — regenerate and it is correct, or CI fails.

531 tests, including a file whose only job is to recompute each published headline from its artifact and assert the result appears in the document that publishes it.

A Limitations section, generated rather than written. Published numbers do not all rest on the same weight of evidence, and a results table cannot show which is which — a correlation across 205 race-seasons and a threshold set by one race print identically. thin_evidence.md walks the artifact behind each headline and counts what sits underneath it, so each number can be weighed on its own evidence.
